# Research Notes: Candice Odgers and the Broader Device-Policy Debate

Updated August 30, 2026.

## Interpretive frame

The scholar's name is **Candice Odgers**. Her work is most useful here as a methodological and equity counterweight to Jonathan Haidt, not as a claim that phones or social media are harmless.

Haidt foregrounds developmental displacement: sleep, play, independence, and face-to-face relationships. Odgers asks whether broad population trends and omnibus measures such as “screen time” can establish causality, whether average associations obscure different experiences across adolescents, and whether offline vulnerabilities shape online risk. These positions can remain in productive tension.

CoSN's 2026 conference recap says Odgers examined youth well-being and technology use through long-term research and gave leaders evidence-based insights for local screen-time conversations. CoSN also emphasized locally driven decision-making, educator judgment, responsible governance, access, and instructional purpose. This supports describing CoSN as **featuring and amplifying** Odgers's evidence-based perspective. It does not establish that CoSN formally endorses every claim she makes.

## What the Odgers evidence supports

- Reviews through 2020 found mixed small positive, negative, and null associations between adolescents' digital technology use and mental health. Most studies were correlational, and stronger designs generally did not establish a large, uniform causal effect.
- Longitudinal and ecological momentary assessment studies strengthen the case for within-person analysis: whether a particular adolescent experiences different symptoms on days of higher use, not merely whether heavier users differ from lighter users.
- Quantity alone is a weak construct. Activity, content, context, timing, purpose, design, and the young person's existing vulnerabilities matter.
- Offline risks often mirror and shape online risks. A policy that restricts access without supplying support can affect adolescents differently and may deepen inequity.
- None of these findings proves that every platform, design practice, high-intensity pattern, or vulnerable subgroup is safe. Small average effects can coexist with concentrated harms and benefits.

## Beyond phones: distinct policy types

The broader policy trend should not be described as a single national or global “rollback.” The evidence documents multiple forms of recalibration:

1. **Personally owned phone restrictions** regulate student access to private devices during the school day.
2. **School-issued device access models** distinguish take-home 1:1, classroom carts, shared devices, limited checkout, and bring-your-own-device approaches.
3. **Instructional-use policies** limit passive video, entertainment, gaming, or cumulative screen exposure while retaining teacher-directed uses.
4. **Analogue alternatives and investments** include physical textbooks, handwriting, paper homework, and non-digital assessments.
5. **Evaluation frameworks** ask whether a proposed use is appropriate, equitable, evidence-based, sustainable, and additive to human teaching.

A classroom cart may change when and where devices are available, but it can still provide one device per student during a lesson. A policy announcement documents what a system intends to change; it is not evidence that learning or well-being improved.
